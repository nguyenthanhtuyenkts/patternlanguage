### Problem
>No one can be close to others, without also having frequent opportunities to be alone.

### Solution
>Give each member of the family a room of their own, especially adults. A minimum room of one’s own is an alcove with a desk, shelves, and curtain. The maximum is a cottage—like a [[154. Teenager's Cottage]] , or an [[155. Old Age Cottage]]. In all cases, especially the adult ones, place these rooms at the far ends of the intimacy gradient—far from the common rooms.

### Related Patterns
... the [[127. Intimacy Gradient]] makes it clear that every house needs rooms where individuals can be alone. In any household which has more than one person, this need is fundamental and essential - [[75. The Family]], [[76. House for a Small Family]], [[77. House for a Couple]]. This pattern, which defines the rooms that people can have to themselves, is the natural counterpart and complement to the social activity provided for in [[129. Common Areas at the Heart]].

Use this pattern as an antidote to the extremes of "togetherness" created by [[129. Common Areas at the Heart]]. Even for small children, give them at least an alcove in the communal sleeping area - [[188. Bed Alcove]]; and for the man and woman, give each of them a separate room, beyond the couples realm they share; it may be an expanded dressing room - [[189. Dressing Rooms|Dressing Room (189)]], a home workshop - [[157. Home Workshop]], or once again, an alcove off some other room - [[179. Alcoves]], [[183. Workspace Enclosure]] - If there is money for it, it may even be possible to give a person a cottage, attached to the main structure - [[154. Teenager's Cottage]], [[155. Old Age Cottage]]. In every case there must at least be room for a desk, a chair, and [[253. Things From Your Life]]. And for the detailed shape of the room, see [[159. Light on Two Sides of Every Room]] and [[191. The Shape of Indoor Space]] ...
